🧱 Structure vs Marks

Rows and Columns build the frame; the Marks card paints inside it. Two different jobs, two different places. Once you can tell which job a drag is doing, you can predict what it will change — the labels and pane count, or the shapes inside them — before you let go.

📖 Terms on this page

Defined before they're used. Every term on the site is also in the glossary.

Header
A text label created by a discrete (blue) field on Rows or Columns. It names a slice of the view.
Example: Category on Columns gives three headers: Furniture, Office Supplies, Technology.
Axis
A numeric scale created by a continuous (green) field on Rows or Columns.
Example: SUM(Sales) on Rows gives a 0–800K axis up the left.
Pane
One rectangle of drawing space, created where the row and column fields meet. Marks live inside panes.
Example: Category on Columns × Region on Rows = 12 panes.
Mark
One drawn thing representing data — a bar, dot, line segment, square or pie wedge.
Example: Sales by Region as bars = four marks.
Encoding
Attaching a field to a visual property of a mark: colour, size, shape, label.
Example: Region on Color encodes region as hue.
Level of detail (of the view)
The combination of every dimension in the view — shelves and Marks card together. It decides how many marks exist.
Example: Category + Region → one mark per pair → 12 marks.

⚖️ Two jobs, two places

Every field you drag is doing one of two things. Not both.

Rows & Columns — build the frame

These shelves carve the sheet up. They decide how many panes there are, where the dividing lines fall, and what the labels down the side and along the bottom say.

  • A blue field makes headers
  • A green field makes an axis
  • More fields here = more panes
→
Marks card — paint inside it

These targets never move a divider or rename a header. They change what the shapes inside each pane look like — and, in the case of Detail, how many of them there are.

  • Color, Size, Shape, Label → appearance
  • Detail → more marks, same look
  • The frame stays exactly where it was
The sentence to memorise

Shelves decide where the boxes are. Marks decide what's drawn in them. If a drag changed the labels or the number of boxes, you touched the frame. If it changed colour, size, or how many shapes sit inside a box, you touched the marks.

🔬 Anatomy of a view

One chart: Category on Columns, SUM(Sales) on Rows. Six named parts — and only one of them is marks.

1 2 3 4 5 6
  1. 1Axis — made by the green measure on Rows. Chrome, not data.
  2. 2Axis title — the field's name. Rename or hide it freely; no mark changes.
  3. 3Header — made by the blue dimension on Columns. One per value.
  4. 4Pane — the drawing space under one header. Marks are confined to it.
  5. 5Mark — the bar itself. This is the only part the Marks card controls.
  6. 6Gridline — worksheet formatting. Delete it and your data is untouched.
Five of the six are chrome

Axis, axis title, header, pane boundary and gridline are all scaffolding — Tableau draws them to hold your data, but they aren't your data. Restyling any of them is a formatting job and the Marks card has nothing to say about it.

⊞ Where the dimensions meet

Put a dimension on both Rows and Columns and you get a grid. Every intersection is a pane, and each pane gets its own marks drawn from just the rows of data belonging to that combination.

Columns → vertical splits

Each value gets a slice of width. Headers run along the top.

Rows → horizontal splits

Each value gets a slice of height. Headers run down the left.

Both → a grid of panes

Every combination gets its own little chart. This is small multiples.

Nesting, not crossing

Two dimensions on the same shelf nest — Category then Region on Columns gives Furniture ▸ Central, Furniture ▸ East, … , one long strip of 12. Two dimensions on opposite shelves cross into the 3 × 4 grid above. Same 12 marks either way; the order of the pills is what changes the shape.

🎯 One field, two destinations

Same data, same field, same twelve marks — dropped in two different places. Only the destination differs, and it changes the chart completely.

Identical numbers, different question

Both charts contain the same twelve values. The left one answers "which region sells most Technology?" — easy, the bars share a baseline. The right one answers "how big is Technology overall, and who makes it up?" — easy, the totals are whole bars. Neither is more correct; the destination you choose is the question you're asking.

Note the axis rescaled: the left chart tops out around one region's sales, the right around a category total. Tableau always fits the axis to the marks it has to draw.

🔢 The mark-count rule

One rule tells you how many marks any view will contain, before you build it:

One mark per combination of dimensions

Tableau draws one mark per distinct combination of every dimension in the view — those on Rows, Columns, and those on Color, Size, Shape, Label and Detail. Measures don't add marks; they only decide how far each mark stretches or how it's coloured.

Marks — wherever the pills sit

Watch the count change as a view gets built. The number in Tableau's bottom-left status bar is the ground truth — check it whenever a chart surprises you.

Adding a dimension changes your numbers too

Aggregates are computed per mark. A view showing AVG(Profit) by Category gives three averages; add Region to Detail and you get twelve, each averaged over fewer rows. The chart didn't "break" — you asked a finer question. This is exactly the trap that LOD expressions exist to solve.

🚫 What is not a mark

Plenty of things on screen look like part of the chart but are drawn by the worksheet, not by your data — which is why the Marks card has no effect on them.

✅ Marks — drawn from data
  • Bars, lines, areas
  • Dots, circles, shapes
  • Squares in a heatmap
  • Pie wedges
  • Map polygons and pins
  • The numbers in a crosstab (mark type Text)
→
❌ Chrome — drawn by the worksheet
  • Axis lines, ticks, tick labels
  • Axis titles and headers
  • Field labels on the shelves
  • Gridlines, zero lines, pane borders
  • Legends, titles, captions
  • Reference lines, trend lines, annotations
The filter test

Not sure which side something falls on? Ask what happens if you filter its data away. Marks disappear — filter out Technology and that bar is gone. Chrome doesn't: the axis, the gridlines and the axis title are all still sitting there. Hover is another giveaway — marks produce a tooltip, chrome doesn't.

Reference and trend lines are the edge case: they're calculated from your data but they aren't marks. They live on the Analytics pane, they don't appear in the mark count, and the Marks card can't style them.

🎨 Where formatting actually lives

Three separate control surfaces, and reaching for the wrong one is the usual reason "I can't find where to change this". Restyling a header or an axis is never a Marks card job.

🔤 Headers & axes

Right-click the axis → Format (fonts, shading, alignment) or Edit Axis (range, title, tick spacing). Right-click a header → Format.

Changes: label text, font, size, number format, tick marks, axis range.

🎯 The marks themselves

The Marks card. Click Color / Size / Label / Shape, or the Color target's Edit Colors, border and halo options.

Changes: fill, opacity, borders, size, symbol, printed labels.

🖼️ Worksheet chrome

Format menu → Lines, Borders, Shading. Plus title, caption and legend formatting.

Changes: gridlines, zero lines, pane dividers, background.

Formatting the frame never touches the marks

Making the axis numbers bold, switching a header to italics, renaming an axis title or hiding tick marks changes zero marks. The bars are the same bars. Watch the mark count in the status bar while you do it — it won't move. Formatting the frame and encoding the data are different activities that happen to sit in the same window.

Two axes means two Marks cards

Build a dual-axis chart and the Marks card splits: an All card plus one per measure. Each controls only its own marks — that's how you make one measure a bar and the other a line. If a colour change isn't landing, check you're on the right card.

🥞 Split or stack? Why Color behaves differently

Drop a dimension on Color and what happens next depends on the mark type — which surprises people who expect Color to only ever recolour things.

Mark typeDimension on Color does…Why
BarStacks segments inside each paneBars share a baseline, so Tableau piles them up rather than overlapping them
LineSplits into one line per valueLines can coexist in a pane without hiding each other
Circle / ShapeRecolours the dots, adding marks if it's a new dimensionPoints are positioned by their axes, so colour is free
AreaStacks bandsSame baseline logic as bars
Square (heatmap)Recolours cellsPosition is already fixed by the row/column headers
Stacked bars hide comparisons

Only the bottom segment of a stack shares a baseline, so the others are genuinely hard to compare — your eye has to judge lengths that start in different places. If the question is "which region is biggest?", move the field from Color to Columns and give every bar the same starting line. If the question is "what's the total, and roughly how is it split?", stacking is the right call. Turn stacking off entirely under Analysis → Stack Marks.

🔧 Symptom → cause → fix

Nearly every "why is my chart doing that?" reduces to a field sitting in the wrong one of the two places.

SymptomCauseFix
Scatter plot shows one dotNo dimension in the view, so all rows aggregate togetherPut a dimension on Detail
Bar chart is one giant barNothing partitioning the viewPut a dimension on Columns or Rows
Got stacks, wanted side-by-side barsDimension is on Color with a bar markMove it to Columns (or turn off Stack Marks)
Marks split but no legend appearedField is on Detail, which adds granularity without encodingMove it to Color if you want it readable
Averages changed after adding a fieldFiner level of detail → each mark averages fewer rowsExpected. Use an LOD expression to pin the grain
View is slow, thousands of marksToo many dimensions in the viewRemove one, or filter before you aggregate
Restyled the axis, marks unchangedWorking as designed — the axis is chromeNothing to fix. Use the Marks card for marks
Colour change won't applyEditing the wrong Marks card on a dual axisSelect the card for that measure, not All

🧠 Key intuitions

  • Rows and Columns build the frame; the Marks card paints inside it. Every drag is one job or the other.If labels or pane count changed, you moved the frame.
  • Blue makes headers, green makes an axis. That's a structural difference, not a colour preference.
  • A pane is where the row and column fields meet. Marks never escape their pane.
  • Marks = one per combination of every dimension in the view, shelves and Marks card alike. Measures never add marks.
  • The same field on Columns vs on Color gives the same mark count arranged two different ways — pick by the question you're asking.
  • Detail is the odd target: more marks, no visual change. Everything else changes appearance.
  • Axes, headers, titles, gridlines and legends are chrome. Restyling them touches no marks and the count never moves.
  • If a chart surprises you, read the mark count in the status bar before changing anything.

🎯 Practice exercises

Do these in order on Superstore, watching the bottom-left mark count after every single step. That number is the fastest feedback loop in Tableau.

  1. Put SUM(Sales) on Rows alone. Predict the mark count before you look, then check.
  2. Add Category to Columns. Count goes 1 → 3. Name the three new headers and the three new panes.
  3. Drag Region to Color. Count goes 3 → 12 — but how many headers are there now? Say why out loud.
  4. Drag that same Region pill from Color up to Columns. The count stays 12. Describe what changed in one sentence.
  5. Put Region back on Color and Category on Rows instead. Same 12 marks again, third arrangement.
  6. Right-click the Sales axis → Edit Axis and retitle it "Revenue". Confirm the mark count did not move.
  7. Right-click the axis → Format and make the numbers bold. Confirm again — two visible changes to the chart, zero changes to the data drawn in it.
  8. Switch the mark type from Automatic to Square, then Line. Note which changes are structural and which are cosmetic.
  9. Build a 3 × 4 grid: Category on Columns, Region on Rows. Point at one pane and say exactly which rows of data feed it.
  10. Drag Sub-Category to Detail. Predict the new count first (3 × 4 × 17 is wrong — work out why).
  11. Turn on Analysis → Stack Marks → Off with a dimension on Color and a bar mark. Explain what you're now looking at.
  12. Add a reference line for average sales. Confirm the mark count is unchanged, and try to recolour it from the Marks card.
🏆 Challenge: predict before you drop

Ten drags in a row. Before releasing the mouse each time, say out loud "this will change the frame" or "this will change the marks", and predict the new mark count. Where you get it wrong, the status bar tells you immediately, and the gap between your prediction and the real count points at which part of the model you've got backwards.
