🔌 Connecting Data

Before you can visualise anything you have to get the data in — and get its shape right. Joins, relationships, unions, live vs extract: these decisions are made once at the start and quietly determine whether your numbers are correct for the rest of the project.

📖 Terms on this page

Defined before they're used. Every term on the site is also in the glossary.

Data source
Your connection to the data, plus any joins and renames made on the Data Source page.
Example: The bundled Superstore file, with its Orders, People and Returns tables.
📍 Bottom-left tab labelled Data Source
Granularity
How finely the data is broken up — what one row represents.
Example: One Superstore row is one product within one order, not one order.
Join
Merging two tables side by side into one wide table, matched on a key.
Example: Attaching each order's returned/not-returned flag from the Returns table.
Relationship
The modern alternative: Tableau keeps the tables separate and works out the join per chart.
Example: The default when you drag a second table onto the canvas.
Union
Stacking tables on top of each other to add rows, not columns.
Example: Twelve monthly CSVs combined into one table of the year.
Fan-out
When a join multiplies rows and inflates your totals.
Example: One monthly target joined to 2,000 order rows repeats 2,000 times — SUM(Target) comes out 2,000× too big.
Extract
A compressed local snapshot of the data instead of a live query. Faster, but needs refreshing.
Example: Tableau Public requires one, which is why publishing converts your connection.

🔗 What Tableau can connect to

📄 Files

Excel, CSV, JSON, PDF, spatial files, statistical files. What you'll use while learning.

🗄️ Databases

SQL Server, PostgreSQL, MySQL, Snowflake, BigQuery, Redshift. What you'll use at work.

☁️ Cloud services

Google Sheets, Salesforce, Google Analytics — plus a generic web-data connector.

Tableau Public limitation: the free version connects to files, Google Sheets and a few web sources — not to databases. That's fine for everything in this course, and the concepts transfer unchanged to Tableau Desktop.

🍰 The canvas has two layers, and they do different things

The Data Source page looks like one canvas but it is two, stacked. Knowing which one you're on explains why dragging a table sometimes produces a flexible noodle and sometimes a join dialog.

Rounded boxes with noodles means you're in the logical layer. Square boxes with Venn-diagram icons means you've gone down into a table's physical layer — and that's where a join can start multiplying your rows.

Logical layer — the top one

What you see when the page opens. Tables sit as separate rounded boxes joined by noodles.

  • Each table keeps its own granularity
  • Tableau writes the join per visualisation
  • Nothing is combined until a sheet asks for it
→
Physical layer — double-click a table

Opens inside that box. Here you build traditional joins and unions with Venn-diagram icons.

  • Tables are flattened into one wide table, immediately
  • The result becomes a single box in the logical layer
  • Fan-out happens here, and only here
Which layer should I be on?

Stay in the logical layer unless you have a reason not to. The physical layer is for when you genuinely need a flattened row-level table — an inner join to filter one table by another, a union of identically-shaped files, or a join Tableau's relationship logic can't express. Everything else is better as a relationship.

Workbooks built before 2020.2 have no logical layer — every table is joined in the physical layer of a single box. That's why old workbooks and old tutorials talk only about joins. Opening one shows a single table on the canvas; double-click it to see the joins underneath.

🪢 Relationships — the modern default

Since 2020.2, dragging a second table onto the canvas creates a relationship (a flexible noodle line) rather than a join. This is the default, and usually the right choice.

✓ What a relationship does

  • Keeps tables separate and at their own granularity
  • Tableau writes the right join per visualisation, based on the fields you use
  • No duplicated rows from one-to-many fan-out
  • Aggregates stay correct automatically

When to use it

Almost always. Especially when tables have different granularity — Orders (one row per line item) related to Returns (one row per returned order), or to Targets (one row per region per month).

What relationships changed

Before relationships, connecting a monthly Targets table to line-item Orders would duplicate every target across thousands of rows, and SUM(Target) would come out wildly inflated with no error to warn you. The only fixes were to pre-aggregate the tables to matching granularity or to write de-duplicating calculations by hand. Relationships remove that work by keeping the tables apart until the moment a specific view needs them combined.

⛓️ Joins — when you need row-level control

Double-click a table on the canvas to open the physical layer, where you can build a traditional join. Joins merge tables into one flat table immediately, before any visualisation.

Inner
Only rows matching in both tables. Silently drops unmatched rows — check your row count.
Left
Everything from the left table, matches from the right, Null where none. The safe default.
Right
The mirror image. Rare — most people swap the tables and use a left join instead.
Full outer
Every row from both sides. Useful for reconciliation — finding what exists in one system but not the other.
Left join is almost always the right starting point

It guarantees you keep every row of your main table. If the join key is broken you'll see Nulls — visible, diagnosable evidence. An inner join in the same situation silently deletes rows, and your totals come out low with no indication why.

The most expensive mistake in Tableau, and it produces numbers that look perfectly reasonable.

SUM(Target) = 1,400. The real total is 900. The West target got counted twice because West had two order rows.

Two order rows, one target row — so the target gets copied onto both. Scale that to 2,000 orders and your target total is 2,000× too big.

Three ways to avoid it

  1. Use a relationship instead of a join

    The modern fix, and the reason relationships exist. Tableau keeps the tables separate and aggregates each at its own granularity. SUM(Target) returns 900.

  2. Or aggregate before joining

    If you must join, roll the finer table up to the coarser table's granularity first so the join is one-to-one.

  3. Or de-duplicate with an LOD

    MIN({FIXED [Region] : MIN([Target])}) pulls one value per region regardless of how many times the row repeats. See LOD Expressions.

Always check the row count after joining

Drop Number of Records (or COUNT(*)) into a view before and after. If it grew, you have fan-out. Superstore's Orders table has 9,994 rows — if it's larger after you connect Returns, something duplicated.

📚 Unions — stacking, not widening

A join adds columns. A union adds rows. Use it when the same data arrives split across several files or tabs — one per month, one per region.

Drag a table below an existing one on the canvas, or right-click → Convert to Union. Use a wildcard union to pick up every sheet or file matching a pattern — new files then join automatically on refresh.

Columns match by name

"Order Date" and "OrderDate" become two separate columns, each half full of Null. Tableau adds a Table Name field showing each row's origin — useful both for debugging and for extracting the month or region encoded in a filename.

🧬 Blending — for data that can't be joined at all

Relationships, joins and unions all need the tables to live in one data source. When they don't — a SQL Server table and a spreadsheet of targets, say — the remaining option is a blend: two separate data sources, combined per sheet, after aggregation.

  • The primary is whichever source you drag from first on that sheet — it gets a blue tick in the Data pane. Every other source is secondary, with an orange one.
  • Linking fields match by name and type automatically. The chain-link icon beside a field shows whether it's active; click it to toggle. Change the pairing under Data → Edit Blend Relationships.
  • The blend is a left join from the primary. Rows in the secondary with no match in the primary are simply absent — which is a quiet way to lose data if you picked the wrong primary.
  • It happens per sheet. Two sheets can blend the same sources differently, which is flexible and makes a workbook harder to reason about.
What you give up by blending
  • Secondary dimensions arrive wrapped in ATTR, so they show * whenever a mark spans more than one value — see ATTR. You cannot use a secondary dimension to add detail to the view.
  • FIXED LOD expressions aren't supported on the secondary source.
  • The linking field must be in the view (or on Detail) for the blend to resolve at the grain you want.
  • Row-level calculations can't cross the two sources — everything is already aggregated by the time they meet.
Decide in this order

Relationship whenever the tables can live in one data source — which, thanks to cross-database joins, is more often than people assume. Join when you need a flat row-level table. Union when it's more rows of the same shape. Blend only when the sources genuinely can't be brought together, or when one is at a much coarser grain and you want it aggregated separately on purpose.

⚡ Live vs Extract

A choice you make on the Data Source page, top right. It affects speed, freshness and what features are available.

Live
  • Queries the source every interaction
  • Always current
  • Speed depends entirely on the database
  • Needs a constant connection

Use when data changes constantly and freshness matters more than speed.

Extract (.hyper)
  • A compressed local snapshot
  • Usually much faster
  • Works offline
  • Needs refreshing to update

Use when the source is slow, remote, or you're publishing to Tableau Public — which requires an extract.

Extracts can be filtered and aggregated

You don't have to extract everything. Edit → Filters limits which rows are pulled; Aggregate data for visible dimensions pre-rolls it up. A 50-million-row table becomes a fast few-thousand-row extract when you only ever look at monthly regional totals.

🧹 Cleaning at the source

Fixes made on the Data Source page apply everywhere, forever. Fixes made in a worksheet apply to that worksheet. Prefer the former.

Rename fields

Double-click a column header. Do this for anything cryptic — the names appear on every axis and tooltip you ever build.

Fix data types

Click the icon above a header. Text that should be a date, numbers stored as strings, IDs that shouldn't be summed.

Hide unused fields

Right-click → Hide. Superstore's Row ID and Country (always "United States") add nothing.

Split columns

Right-click → Split, or Custom Split. Turns "CA-2016-152156" into usable parts without a calculated field.

Data source filters

Top-right → Filters → Add. Excludes rows from everything — test records, cancelled orders, irrelevant years.

Set geographic roles

Right-click → Geographic Role. Needed before a field will work on a map.

Data source filters are invisible later

Unlike a worksheet filter, a data source filter leaves no pill on any shelf and no card beside any view — nothing on screen says it exists. Six months on, someone asks why the totals don't match the source system and the answer is a filter set on this page that nobody remembers. Document them — a text object on the dashboard, or a note in the workbook description.

🔨 Reshaping a file that arrived the wrong way round

Two tools on the Data Source page fix the two most common ways a spreadsheet is unusable. Both take seconds and both are easy to miss because they only appear in the right context.

Data Interpreter

A checkbox in the left panel, offered whenever Tableau suspects the sheet has decoration around the data — a title row, a logo, merged cells, blank spacer rows, footnotes underneath.

Tick it and Tableau finds the real rectangle of data and uses that. Click Review the results and it opens an annotated copy of the workbook showing exactly what it decided to keep and drop — worth doing once so you trust it.

It's a guess, and a good one. Check the row count afterwards regardless.

Pivot — columns into rows

Data arrives wide: one column per month, twelve of them. Tableau wants it long: a Month column and a Value column. Select the twelve column headers in the preview grid, right-click → Pivot.

// Before — 12 columns, unusable as a date
Region | Jan | Feb | Mar | …

// After — 2 columns, one row per month
Region | Pivot Field Names | Pivot Field Values

Rename the two new fields, set the names one to a date type, and you have a time series Tableau can actually chart.

Wide data is the single most common reason a chart "can't be built"

A column per month can't go on a date axis, can't be filtered by year, can't have a running total. No amount of calculating fixes it — the shape is wrong. If you find yourself writing [Jan] + [Feb] + [Mar], stop and pivot instead. The same principle drives Measure Names and Measure Values, which do this reshape on the fly for measures already in the source.

Pivot is available for file-based sources and some databases. Where it's greyed out, do the reshape upstream — Power Query unpivots in two clicks, and a SQL view can do it with UNION ALL.

🧠 Key intuitions

  • Relationships by default — joins only when you need row-level control
  • Joins add columns; unions add rows
  • Left join is the safe join — Nulls are visible, dropped rows aren't
  • Always check the row count after connecting — growth means fan-out
  • Different granularity is the danger — that's where duplication comes from
  • Fix names and types once, at the source
  • Extract for speed, live for freshness — Tableau Public requires an extract
  • Document data source filters — they're invisible from the worksheet
  • Logical layer for relationships, physical layer for joins — double-click a table to go down a level
  • Blend only when the sources can't be combined — it costs you ATTR, FIXED and row-level calcs
  • Wide data has to be pivoted, not calculated around

🎯 Practice exercises

The Superstore workbook ships with three tables: Orders, Returns and People.

  1. Open the Data Source page and note the row count of Orders (it should be 9,994)
  2. Relate Returns to Orders on Order ID, then confirm the row count hasn't changed
  3. Build a view of Sales by Region and confirm the total is still $2,297,201
  4. Now join Returns instead of relating it — check the row count again and explain any change
  5. Create a Returned? calculation flagging orders that appear in Returns
  6. Relate the People table and show Sales by regional manager
  7. Rename three fields to something clearer and hide Row ID and Country
  8. Check every data type, especially the two date fields
  9. Split Order ID on the dash and see what the parts contain
  10. Add a data source filter excluding 2014, then verify your totals drop
  11. Switch the connection to an Extract and note whether interaction feels faster
  12. Add an extract filter for 2016–2017 only and compare the extract's size
  13. Double-click the Orders table to open the physical layer, then come back out — describe what changed on the canvas
  14. Make a small wide spreadsheet (Region plus twelve month columns), connect to it, and pivot it into a usable shape
  15. Connect that spreadsheet as a second data source and blend it to Orders on Region. Put a secondary dimension on Detail and explain the *
🏆 Challenge: build the fan-out yourself

Make a small two-column Targets table by hand — one row per region with a monthly target — and connect it to Orders. First as a join: sum the targets and watch the number come out roughly 2,500 times too large. Then as a relationship: watch it come out right. Finally, build a Sales vs Target view with a bullet chart. The point is to see what the broken version looks like: the inflated total is a plausible-looking number, not an error message, so the only warning sign is the row count.
