📈 Designing Charts

How to build and format a chart in Excel: which type fits which question, what each control in the Format pane does, and where to find it. The studio below runs on real Superstore figures — change a setting and the chart, the critique of it, and the matching Excel menu path all update.

🔬 Anatomy — every part has a name

Excel has no "format the chart" command. It formats whatever is selected, and there are ten different things you can select. A setting you can't find is usually a setting that belongs to an object you haven't selected.

Click any part of the chart below.

The two-click rule

One click on a bar selects the whole series — every bar of that colour. A second click on the same bar narrows the selection to that single data point. Formatting applies to whichever of the two is selected, so the second click is what lets you recolour one bar and leave the rest of the series alone.

🛠️ Getting a chart on the page

Four routes: two keyboard shortcuts and two dialogs.

⌨️ Alt+F1 — instant chart

Select the range, hit it, and Excel drops a default clustered column onto the sheet — no dialog, no choices. Useful while you're still exploring the data.

⌨️ F11 — chart on its own sheet

Same chart, but on a dedicated full-screen chart sheet instead of floating over the data. Good for a chart that is the deliverable. Chart Design ▸ Move Chart moves one either way afterwards.

🧠 Insert ▸ Recommended Charts

Excel reads the shape of your selection and previews a handful of options with your own data in them. The All Charts tab beside it is the full gallery.

⚡ Ctrl+Q — Quick Analysis

A floating menu on the selection with a Charts tab that previews on hover. Windows only, and quicker than the ribbon when your hands are already on the data.

What "select your data" actually means

Chart a Table, not a range

If your data is a real Excel Table (Ctrl+T), the chart's source grows with it. Add fifty rows next month and the chart just includes them. Chart a plain range like A1:B5 and you'll be editing Select Data by hand every time the data grows.

Two buttons worth knowing before you need them

Chart Design ▸ Switch Row/Column transposes what's on the axis and what's in the legend. When a chart is almost right but grouped the wrong way round, this is a single click instead of a rebuild.

The funnel icon beside a selected chart (Windows) hides a series or category from the chart without touching the data — handy for "what does this look like without the outlier?".

🎛️ The chart studio

Every control the rest of this page describes is on this widget. Pick a dataset, pick a chart type, then turn the dials. Four things update as you go:

  • The critique underneath — what a reviewer would say about the chart as it currently stands, including the arithmetic when a setting is distorting something.

🎨 Choosing the type

The type follows from three things: what the reader is asking, how many categories there are, and whether the numbers share a unit. For the full framework — distributions, flows, maps — see the Chart Decision Tree.

Column
Comparing a handful of categories. Up to about eight, with short labels. Sort it. Always start at zero.
Bar (horizontal)
Ranking many categories, or long labels. Seventeen sub-categories fit down the side; across the bottom they wouldn't. Names read straight instead of tilted.
Line
A shape over time. Time runs left to right, always. A line drawn between categories that have no order implies a sequence that isn't there.
Area
A total over time, when volume is the point. The filled area is a quantity, so this one can never have a trimmed axis.
Stacked
Total and composition. Only the bottom segment sits on a straight baseline, so keep it to two or three and put the one you care about at the bottom.
100% stacked
Mix only. Every bar is drawn to the same height, so a region worth $70K and one worth $700K look identical. Use it when share is the whole question.
Scatter
Two numbers per row. Discount vs Profit is where the Superstore's margin turns negative. Note it's Scatter, not Line — Line would treat X as a category.
Combo
Two units at once. Sales as bars, margin % as a line on a secondary axis. Label both axes and colour-match each to its series.
Pie / doughnut
Two to five slices, adding to a meaningful whole. Beyond five, the slices are too close in angle to put in order by eye. Negatives are drawn at their size with the sign dropped.
The one-sentence rule

If you can't say what the chart shows in one sentence, you've picked the wrong chart — or you're trying to show two things at once and need two charts.

📐 The value axis

Bounds — where the axis starts

A bar means its length. A length is only meaningful measured from zero, so the moment you set Format Axis ▸ Bounds ▸ Minimum above zero, every bar's length stops corresponding to its value.

West sells $725K, the South $392K — a gap of 1.85×. The left-hand chart draws it as 4.6×. The numbers are identical in both; only the axis minimum changed. Flip Value axis in the studio and read the exaggeration factor it calculates.

The one exception

A line chart encodes position, not length, so trimming its axis to show a 2% movement is defensible — stock charts do it constantly. Say so on the chart. Bars, columns and areas can't: their length is the measurement.

Display units — six digits on every tick

Format Axis ▸ Display units ▸ Thousands turns 725,458 into 725 and puts a "Thousands" label on the axis. Tick Show display units label on chart, or put the unit in the axis title yourself — “Sales ($000)”.

Try Number format ▸ Thousands in the studio against Full number — the tick labels get shorter, which leaves more of the width for the plot itself.

📊 Series options

Right-click any bar ▸ Format Data Series. Gap Width and Series Overlap are only in this pane — not on the ribbon, not under the ⊕ button.

Gap Width default 219%

The gap between bars, as a percentage of bar width. At 219% each bar takes about a third of its slot. Drop it to 0% and the bars touch — which is the convention for a histogram, i.e. continuous data. Using 0% on separate categories signals continuous data when it isn't.

Sensible range for a category chart: 40–150%. Excel's default is on the thin side.

Series Overlap default −27%

How far bars within one cluster slide over each other. Negative pushes them apart, positive overlaps them. At 100% they'd sit on top of one another — which is exactly what a stacked chart is, and why Excel forces overlap to 100% the moment you stack.

Set it to 0% so clustered bars just touch: the cluster reads as one group rather than four strays.

Both sliders are live in the studio — pick Sales by Region × Category and a clustered column to feel what they do.

The secondary axis

Sales are hundreds of thousands; margin is a number between 0 and 0.2. Share one scale between them and the margin series is drawn a pixel high, flat along the axis.

How: click the small series once to select it (or pick it from Format ▸ Chart Elements dropdown when it's too small to hit) ▸ Format Data Series ▸ Plot Series On ▸ Secondary Axis. Then change that series to a Line via Change Chart Type, so the two measures don't compete as bars.

Two axes means two choices of scale

Because you choose both axes' bounds independently, you can make any two series appear to move together or apart. If you use one, keep both axes on Auto, label both with their units, and match each axis's colour to its series. Otherwise you've built a chart that can be tuned to whatever conclusion you wanted.

Gaps, zeros and hidden rows

Chart Design ▸ Select Data ▸ Hidden and Empty Cells holds three settings people meet by accident:

SituationWhat Excel doesWhat you probably want
Blank cell mid-seriesLeaves a gap in the lineUsually right — a gap is honest about missing data
A cell containing 0Plots a point at zeroOnly if it really was zero. "No data" is not zero.
A formula returning ""Plots it as zero — the line dives to the floorReturn =NA() instead: =IF(cond, value, NA()). Excel skips #N/A in charts.
Rows hidden by a filterDrops them from the chartUsually right, and it's how a filtered Table drives a chart. Tick Show data in hidden rows and columns to override.

🌈 Colour

Excel's default is a different colour per series, and people extend the habit to a different colour per bar. Ask what the colour is encoding. If the answer is "which bar this is", the axis label already said it and the colour adds nothing to read.

1 · Encode a category

Different colours for different series, kept identical across every chart in the report. Furniture is the same blue everywhere or the reader relearns the legend each time.

2 · Encode a quantity

One hue, light to dark. Only for genuinely ordered values — a heat map or a filled map, never a category list.

3 · Encode a sign or a threshold

Red below zero, grey or green above. On the Profit by Sub-Category chart, three bars go red and the loss-makers can be picked out without reading the axis.

4 · Point at the subject

Everything grey, one thing coloured. Two clicks: click the bar to get the series, click again for the single point, then set the fill.

Highlighting one thing

How, for a single bar: click the bar once (selects the series), click it again (selects that point), then Format ▸ Shape Fill. For a line series, select the line and set its colour to a light grey. That's it — no helper columns, no second series.

Red–green colour blindness

Around 8% of men and 0.5% of women can't reliably separate red from green. If colour is the only thing distinguishing two series, those readers lose the distinction entirely. Two fixes: use the Okabe–Ito palette (the studio has it under Colour), and make sure something else — a label, a position, an order — carries the same information.

Chart Design ▸ Change Colors switches the whole palette, and Page Layout ▸ Colors changes the workbook theme so every chart in the file moves together. Build the theme once at the start rather than recolouring eleven charts at the end.

🏷️ Data labels

Gridlines let a reader estimate a value. Labels give it to them exactly. You rarely want both, and you almost never want a label on every point.

Label everything

Fine up to about eight bars. Then delete the gridlines — they're now repeating what the labels say.

Label the point you're making

Click twice to select one label, keep it, delete the rest. Best default for a chart with a message.

Label the last point

On a line chart, a label at the right-hand end doubles as the series name and lets you delete the legend entirely.

Value From Cells

The one label feature worth going out of your way for. Format Data Labels ▸ Label Options ▸ Label Contains ▸ Value From Cells lets a label show the contents of any range — not just the plotted number.

So you can build a helper column of text and put it on the chart:

=IF([@Profit]<0, "⚠ loss", TEXT([@Profit],"$#,##0"))   // conditional label text
=TEXT([@Growth],"+0.0%;-0.0%") & " vs LY"       // annotate with a second measure

This is how you annotate a chart without floating text boxes that drift out of place the moment someone resizes it.

SettingEffect
Label Position ▸ Outside EndAbove the bar. Cleanest for columns; needs headroom, so raise the axis Max slightly if labels clip.
Label Position ▸ Inside EndInside the bar tip. The only sensible option for stacked charts.

📅 The category axis

Text axis vs Date axis — what happens to missing months

If your labels are real dates, Format Axis ▸ Axis Type offers two behaviours, and they tell different stories from identical data:

Date axis

Spaces points by real elapsed time. A month with no orders leaves a visible hole, and a jump from January to June looks like a jump from January to June.

Text axis

Gives every label an equal slot. January and June end up adjacent and equally spaced — the missing months vanish, so the line implies a continuous series the data doesn't have.

Rule: if the axis is time, use a Date axis, and let the gaps show. Use a Text axis only when your labels are grouping names — "Q1", "FY24", "Week 1" — that aren't dates at all.

Sort order, and the upside-down bar chart

A chart has no sort of its own. It draws the source range in the order the range is in — so you sort the data, not the chart (Data ▸ Sort, or the Table header dropdown).

Then the order comes out backwards

Excel plots the first row of a bar (horizontal) chart at the bottom. So you sort high-to-low, and the chart comes out climbing — biggest at the bottom, smallest at the top. Fix it with Format Axis ▸ Categories in reverse order, not by re-sorting your data backwards. The studio has that exact checkbox: pick Profit by Sub-Category, a bar chart, sort High → low, then toggle it.

Ticking it also flings the value axis to the top of the chart. Format Axis ▸ Horizontal axis crosses ▸ At maximum category puts it back at the bottom.

Long labels

When names don't fit, Excel tilts them to 45° or stacks them vertically. Both are a symptom: the names are wider than the slot a column chart gives each category. Switch to a bar chart and every label reads straight, with as much room as it needs. Try it in the studio — Profit by Sub-Category as a column chart tilts all seventeen names; as a bar chart it's immediately readable.

⚡ Sparklines — a chart inside a cell

Word-sized charts that live in the cell background, so a 20-row table can carry 20 trends without 20 charts.

Insert: select the cells where you want them ▸ Insert ▸ Sparklines ▸ Line / Column / Win-Loss ▸ choose the data range. A Sparkline tab appears while one is selected.

Line

The default. Best for a continuous trend.

Column

Discrete periods, and negatives read clearly.

Win/Loss

Sign only, no magnitude. Up months vs down months.

Set the axis, or the rows aren't comparable

By default each sparkline scales to its own data, so a row going 1 → 2 looks identical to one going 100 → 200. When you're comparing rows, set Sparkline ▸ Axis ▸ Vertical Axis Minimum/Maximum ▸ Same for All Sparklines. It's the same problem as the value axis above: a shared scale is what makes two rows comparable.

Sparkline ▸ Show adds a marker on the High Point, Low Point and Last Point — with a group of sparklines, high and low points let a reader scan the column and find the interesting rows without reading any numbers.

Sparklines aren't shapes; they're a property of the cell, so you can't click and delete one. Use Sparkline ▸ Clear. Text typed in the cell shows on top of the sparkline, which is a nice trick: put the current value in the cell and the trend behind it.

Google Sheets has no Sparkline tab — it has a worksheet function instead:

=SPARKLINE(B2:M2)                          // line
=SPARKLINE(B2:M2, {"charttype","column"}) // column
=SPARKLINE(B2:M2, {"charttype","winloss";"color","#4e79a7"})

🔄 Charts that update themselves

Four ways to keep a chart's source growing with the data, cheapest first.

1 · Chart a Table

Rows added to a Table are inside the chart's source automatically. One keystroke — Ctrl+T — and the chart's range stops being something you maintain.

2 · Link the title to a cell

Select the chart title, type = in the formula bar and click a cell. Build that cell's text with a formula and the title restates the finding as the data changes.

3 · Pivot chart + slicers

Chart a pivot and it inherits the pivot's grouping, sorting and filters. Insert ▸ Slicer and Insert ▸ Timeline give readers buttons; Report Connections wires one slicer to every pivot at once.

4 · A staging range

Chart a small block of cells fed by SORT, FILTER or TAKE. The chart stays simple; the formulas decide what's in it.

A self-sorting top-10 chart, using dynamic arrays to feed the chart's range:

=TAKE(SORT(UNIQUE(Orders[State]), 1, 1), 10)          // labels
=SUMIF(Orders[State], H2#, Orders[Sales])                // values, spilled alongside
The spill-range catch

Excel charts still won't take a spill reference like H2# as a source. Point the chart at a generous fixed range ($H$2:$I$40) that the spill sits inside, and set Hidden and Empty Cells so the unused rows don't plot — return =NA() rather than "" from the padding.

Laying charts out on a dashboard

Hold Alt while dragging or resizing a chart and it snaps to the cell grid — the way to get six charts aligned without nudging them. Ctrl+drag duplicates one, so build the first chart properly and copy it rather than formatting each from scratch. More on this in the Dashboard Project.

❌ 3D effects

Perspective draws the front series larger than the back one at identical values.

❌ A legend for one series

It names a colour that means nothing. Select it and press Delete; the title already says what's being measured.

❌ Doughnut holes full of nothing

If you're using a doughnut, put the total in the middle. Otherwise it's a pie with less room for labels.

❌ "Chart 1" as a title

Restating the axis labels tells the reader nothing the axes don't. Put the finding there instead.

❌ Dual axes tuned to agree

Two independent scales can be set to make any pair of series look correlated. Keep both on Auto.

❌ Every category a different colour

The colour repeats what the axis label already says.

✅ The 60-second critique

Run this before a chart leaves your screen. It's the same list the studio's critique panel works from.

QuestionIf the answer is no
1Can I say what this chart shows in one sentence?It's answering two questions. Split it into two charts.
2Does the title say the finding rather than the fields?Rewrite it. "West leads by $334K" beats "Sales by Region".
3Does the value axis start at zero?Unless it's a line chart and you've said so, put it back.
4Is it sorted, if it's a ranking?Sort the source. Then check a bar chart isn't upside down.
5Does the colour encode something?Go to one colour, or grey plus one highlight.
6Can a reader recover a value?Add gridlines or labels — one of the two, rarely both.
7Do both axes carry their units?Add them, including the display unit: "Sales ($000)".
8Is anything on the chart doing no work?Delete it. Legends of one, redundant gridlines, borders, drop shadows.

10Did someone else get it in five seconds?Believe them, not yourself. You already know what it says.

🎯 Practice

In the studio — these need no spreadsheet, and each one has a specific answer:

  1. Load “The misleading one”. Note the exaggeration factor, then fix it in one click and watch it disappear.
  2. Load “The 17-slice pie” and get to a chart that answers "which sub-categories lose money?" in under five seconds.
  3. On Profit by Sub-Category, make a bar chart sorted high to low — then fix the fact that it reads upside down without touching the sort.
  4. On Sales by Region × Category, find the gap width and overlap that make the clusters read as four groups rather than twelve strays.
  5. Put Sales by Region on a 100% stacked chart and write down what question it can no longer answer.
  6. On Discount vs Profit, turn on the trendline and explain what the R² is telling you — and what it isn't.
  7. Take any chart and set the title to something a manager could act on. The critique panel checks the title along with everything else.

🏆 Challenge: one chart, one decision
